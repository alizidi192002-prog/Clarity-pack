How Much Should Freelancers Set Aside for Taxes? A Simple Percentage Method

Updated 2026-10-19 · 6 min read

When you work for an employer, tax comes out of your pay before you ever see it. As a freelancer, the whole payment lands in your account and the tax bill arrives months later. The fix is simple: move a fixed percentage of every payment into a separate account the day it arrives, and never treat that money as yours.

Why freelancers get caught out

The problem is rarely that freelancers earn too little to pay their tax. It's timing. Money comes in during the year, gets spent on rent, groceries and equipment, and then a single large bill shows up at the end. On top of income tax, many self-employed people also pay their own social contributions. In the US, for example, self-employment tax (Social Security and Medicare) is 15.3% on most net self-employment earnings, and it's charged on top of income tax. Other countries have their own versions. Either way, the total can be noticeably more than people expect.

Setting money aside as you go turns one scary bill into lots of small, boring transfers. Boring is good.

Step 1: Pick a starting percentage

There's no single right number, because it depends on where you live, how much you earn and what you can deduct. A common guideline is to set aside 25% to 30% of what you earn as a freelancer. Treat that as a starting point, not a rule:

  • Lower income, lots of business expenses: the lower end (or below) may be enough.
  • Higher income or few deductions: lean toward 30% or more.
  • Not sure at all: pick the higher number. Ending the year with money left in the tax account is a nice surprise. Being short is not.

Step 2: Check it against last year (if you can)

If you've already filed a tax return as a freelancer, you can work out your own real percentage. Take the total tax you owed for the year (income tax plus any self-employment or social contributions) and divide it by your profit for that year.

Last yearAmount
Freelance income$42,000
Business expenses$3,600
Profit (income − expenses)$38,400
Total tax owed$9,600
Your real rate ($9,600 ÷ $38,400)25%

If your income is going up this year, round up by a few points, because tax rates often rise as income rises. If it's going down, you can keep the same rate and enjoy the extra cushion.

Step 3: Set aside from every single payment

The method only works if it's automatic. Every time a client pays you, move your percentage into a separate savings account before you spend anything. Here's what a quarter looks like at 25%:

$8,400 × 25% = $2,100 sitting in the tax account at the end of the quarter, and $6,300 left to live on and run the business. That $6,300 is the number your personal budget should be built on, not the $8,400.

Tip: setting aside from the full payment (not from profit) is slightly more than you strictly need, because your expenses will reduce what you owe. That small overshoot is a built-in safety margin. If the account ends the year with extra in it, move it to your emergency fund.

Step 4: Use a separate account, and name it

Keeping tax money in your everyday account is how it disappears. Open a separate savings account (ideally one that pays some interest) and give it a clear name like "TAX — do not touch". Many banks let you create multiple pots or sub-accounts for free. The goal is simple: when you look at your main balance, every dollar you see is money you can actually use.

Step 5: Know your payment dates

Many tax systems expect self-employed people to pay during the year, not just once at the end. In the US, estimated tax payments are usually due four times a year (around mid-April, mid-June, mid-September and mid-January). In the UK, Self Assessment can include payments on account in January and July. Look up the rules for your country, write the dates in your calendar, and check that the tax account will have enough in it before each one.

Missing these dates can mean penalties or interest, so this is worth ten minutes of research. If your situation is complicated, a local accountant can tell you exactly what applies to you.

Step 6: Review every quarter

Once a quarter, compare what's in the tax account with what you expect to owe. Three quick checks:

  • Is income higher than planned? Consider raising your percentage for the rest of the year.
  • Have you had big deductible expenses? You may be able to lower it slightly.
  • Did you skip any transfers? Catch up now, while the gap is small.

What about VAT or sales tax?

If you charge VAT or sales tax, that money was never yours. It belongs to the tax office from the moment the client pays. Move the full VAT or sales tax amount from each invoice into the tax account too, separately from your income tax percentage, so you're not surprised when that return is due.

Make it part of your budget

Freelance income is uneven, so your tax set-aside should sit right next to your other money rules. If your income changes a lot month to month, read our guide on how to budget on irregular income, which shows how to pay yourself a steady "salary" from a buffer. Tax comes off first, then the buffer, then your budget.

Taxes vary a lot by country and personal situation, so treat this as general information, not tax or financial advice. The habit itself, though, works almost everywhere: a fixed percentage, a separate account, every payment, no exceptions.
