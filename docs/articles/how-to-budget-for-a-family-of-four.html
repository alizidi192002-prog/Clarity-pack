How to Budget for a Family of Four: A Monthly Plan With Real Numbers

Updated 2026-11-27 · 7 min read

With two adults and two kids, money leaves the account in a lot of small directions at once: school trips, shoes that stop fitting, a birthday party every other weekend. A family budget works when it plans for that mess instead of pretending each month looks the same.

This guide walks through a simple way to build a monthly budget for a family of four, with a full worked example. Your numbers will be different (housing and childcare costs vary hugely by city), so treat the example as a shape to copy, not a target.

1. Start with your real take-home pay

Add up what actually lands in your bank account each month after tax, retirement contributions and any health insurance taken from your paycheck. If one or both of you are paid weekly or every two weeks, use the amount from a normal month (four weekly paychecks, or two biweekly ones) as your baseline. The extra paychecks that some months bring can go straight to goals.

If either income is irregular, such as commission, tips or freelance work, use the lowest normal month as the baseline. Anything above it is a bonus you assign once it arrives.

2. List the fixed costs first

Fixed costs are the bills that stay roughly the same every month: rent or mortgage, insurance, phone and internet, loan payments, and childcare or after-school care. Write them down with exact amounts from your statements. These are the hardest to change quickly, so they set the frame for everything else.

For many families, childcare is the second-biggest line after housing. If you pay for daycare or after-school care, check whether your employer offers a dependent care account or whether you qualify for a childcare tax credit where you live. Rules differ by country and state, so check the official source or a tax professional.

3. Give groceries a realistic number

Groceries are where family budgets most often break. Kids eat more as they grow, and school lunches and snacks add up. Look at the last three months of grocery spending, take the average, and use that as your starting point rather than a hopeful lower number.

Then cut it on purpose, not by willpower. Planning dinners for the week, shopping from a list and cooking a double batch twice a week usually does more than clipping coupons. Our guide to meal planning to save money on groceries has a simple weekly routine you can use.

4. Build the monthly budget

Here is a full example for a family of four with a combined take-home pay of $6,200 a month. Every dollar has a job, so income minus spending and saving comes to zero.

In this example, needs (housing, utilities, groceries, transport, insurance, childcare and phones) come to $4,950, or about 80% of take-home pay. That's higher than the common 50/30/20 guideline suggests, and that is normal for many families with young children. The guideline is a starting point, not a rule you've failed. You can test your own split with the free 50/30/20 calculator.

5. Set up sinking funds for the costs that aren't monthly

The $300 sinking fund line is what keeps the family budget from falling apart in August and December. A sinking fund is money you set aside each month for a cost you know is coming but don't pay monthly. Go through the year and list them:

Irregular expenseWhenPer year
Christmas & holidaysDecember$900
Family birthdays (4 × $75)Through the year$300
Back to schoolAugust–September$400
Car repairs & registrationAs needed$900
Summer camp or family tripJune–August$1,100
Total$3,600

$3,600 ÷ 12 = $300 a month. Keep this money in a separate savings account (or sub-accounts if your bank offers them) so it doesn't get spent on everyday things by accident. When the back-to-school list arrives, the money is already there.

6. Decide who tracks what

In a two-adult household, a budget fails quietly when nobody owns it. Pick one simple system:

  • One shared account for all household bills and groceries, with each adult's pay going in.
  • A small personal amount for each adult that needs no explanation. Even $50 each a month cuts down on arguments about small purchases.
  • One person updates the spreadsheet weekly, but both look at it together once a month.

If you and your partner earn very different amounts, see our guide on how couples can split expenses for fair ways to divide shared costs.

7. Review once a month as a family

Set a 20-minute check-in at the end of each month. Compare what you planned with what you spent in each category, move money between categories if one went over, and check progress on your savings and debt goals. A useful rule of thumb: if a category runs over for two months in a row, the budget was too low. Raise it and take the money from somewhere else, rather than hoping next month will be different.

Older kids can join part of the conversation. Showing them that the trip fund is at $800 of $1,100 teaches more about money than any lecture.

Common family budget mistakes

  • Forgetting the small recurring costs. Streaming services, app subscriptions, school lunch top-ups and sports fees add up. Check your statements for anything that repeats.
  • No emergency fund. With kids, something always breaks. Even a starter fund of one month's essential costs gives you breathing room.
  • Budgeting the "ideal" month. Use real averages, especially for groceries and fuel.
  • Skipping fun entirely. A budget with zero for fun usually lasts a few weeks. A small, planned amount is easier to stick to.
Tip: set up your family budget in December. Listing next year's birthdays, school dates and holidays before January means the sinking funds start filling from the first paycheck of the year.
