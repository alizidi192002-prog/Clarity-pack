Year-End Money Checklist: 10 Things to Do Before December 31

Updated 2026-10-22 · 6 min read

The last weeks of the year are the best time to look back at your money. Your statements cover a full twelve months, some deadlines are about to pass, and a fresh budget is easier to start on January 1 than in March. This checklist takes one or two quiet evenings.

Work through it in order. The first few steps tell you what happened this year; the rest turn that into a plan for next year.

1. Total up your year in numbers

Download your bank and card statements for the year (or the last 10 months if December isn't over yet, then estimate the rest). Add up three things: total take-home income, total spending, and what's left. Then group spending into a handful of broad categories. You don't need perfect precision; rounded numbers are fine for a review.

Here's an example for someone who took home $42,000 in 2026:

Seeing the whole year in one table is often surprising. In this example, food and eating out together come to $9,600, more than half of what rent costs. That isn't wrong, but it's a useful thing to know before you set next year's budget.

2. Work out your savings rate

Your savings rate is what you saved divided by your take-home income. In the example, $4,200 ÷ $42,000 = 10%. It's one simple number you can compare from year to year. Whether 10% is "enough" depends on your goals, income and stage of life, so treat any rule of thumb you've heard as a guideline, not a test you pass or fail. The useful question is: did it go up or down compared to last year, and why?

3. Take a net worth snapshot

Write down what you own (cash, savings, investments, the rough value of a car) and what you owe (card balances, loans). Assets minus debts is your net worth. Do it on the same date every year, for example December 31, and you'll have a clear picture of progress that a single month's budget can't show.

4. List every debt with its rate

For each debt, note the balance, the interest rate and the minimum payment. Seeing them side by side makes it easier to choose which one to attack first in the new year. 5. Check year-end deadlines

Some money rules reset on January 1, and the details depend on your country and employer. Before the year closes, it's worth checking:

  • Workplace benefits with a "use it or lose it" balance, such as some health or flexible spending accounts.
  • Yearly contribution limits on retirement or tax-advantaged savings accounts, and whether you still have room this year.
  • Paid leave that expires if you don't take it.
  • Any charitable giving you want to count in this tax year.

Rules vary a lot, so check your own plan documents or ask a qualified tax adviser if you're unsure.

6. Gather your tax paperwork now

Create one folder (paper or digital) for the year: payslips, interest statements, receipts for anything deductible, and invoices if you do freelance work. Filling it in December is far easier than searching your inbox in spring.

7. Cancel what you didn't use

Scroll through the "Bills" and "Other" lines of your statements and highlight every recurring charge: streaming, apps, memberships, cloud storage. If you didn't use something in the last three months, cancel it before it renews. Even a few small cuts add up: three $12 subscriptions are $432 a year.

8. Mark next year's renewal dates

Note when insurance, annual subscriptions, licences and memberships renew, and roughly what each costs. These irregular bills are what break most monthly budgets. Once you know the months, you can save a little toward each one every month instead of being caught out.

9. Top up or reset your emergency fund

If you dipped into your emergency savings this year, make refilling it one of your first goals for January. If it's intact, check whether it still matches your current monthly costs, since rent or bills may have gone up during the year.

10. Set up your 2027 budget

Now turn the review into a plan. Use this year's real totals as your starting point, not last January's hopes. In the example, holidays and gifts cost $1,200, so a $100-a-month line for gifts goes straight into the new budget. Pick two or three specific goals, divide each by 12, and add them as fixed lines. Tip: book a 30-minute "money date" in your calendar for the last week of December. If you share finances, do it together, and pick one thing each of you wants to change next year.

None of this needs to be perfect. A rough review that you actually finish is worth more than a detailed one you never start. These guides are general information, not financial advice; your own situation may call for different choices.
