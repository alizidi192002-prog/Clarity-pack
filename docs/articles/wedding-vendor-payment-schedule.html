Wedding Vendor Payment Schedule: When Deposits and Final Payments Are Due

Updated 2026-10-30 · 7 min read

A wedding budget tells you how much you'll spend. A payment schedule tells you when. Most couples know their total, but get caught out because a big share of it is due in the final weeks. Put every deposit and balance on one timeline and you can save toward each date instead of scrambling.

Why the timing matters as much as the total

Wedding vendors rarely ask for the full price up front. A typical contract asks for a deposit to hold the date, sometimes a second payment a few months out, and the balance shortly before the wedding. That sounds manageable, until you notice that the venue, caterer, florist and DJ often want their balances in the same two-week window. If your savings are on track for the total but not for that window, you end up putting thousands on a credit card at the worst possible moment.

A payment schedule fixes this. It's simply a list of every amount you owe, sorted by due date, with a running total so you can see how much cash you need by each point.

Step 1: Collect the payment terms from every contract

For each vendor, write down five things: the total contract price, the deposit amount, any interim payments, the final balance, and the exact due date of each. Contracts usually say "balance due 14 days before the event" or "50% at signing" rather than giving a calendar date, so convert each one into a real date as soon as you sign.

While you're reading the contract, also note:

  • Whether the deposit is refundable and what happens if you change the date or cancel.
  • Extras not in the headline price: service charges, tax, delivery, overtime and gratuities can add a noticeable amount, so ask each vendor whether these are already included.
  • How they accept payment, and whether card payments carry a fee.
  • Headcount deadlines: caterers often fix the final bill from the guest count you confirm a few weeks out, so that balance can move up or down.

Step 2: Lay out each vendor's payments

Here's an example for a $20,000 wedding. The amounts and percentages are illustrative; your vendors' terms will differ, which is exactly why you write them down.

The timeline makes the crunch obvious: $8,750 is due in a single two-week window, and $10,500 ($8,750 + $1,750) falls in the final two weeks and just after. That's the number your savings plan has to be ready for.

Step 4: Check your savings against each date

Compare your running total with how much you'll have saved by each point. Say this couple starts with $6,000 already saved and adds $1,200 a month for the 12 months before the wedding:

  • 12 months before: $6,000 saved vs $3,400 due. Covered.
  • 3 months before (9 more months of saving): $6,000 + $10,800 = $16,800 saved vs $9,500 due. Covered, with $7,300 left.
  • Wedding: $6,000 + $14,400 = $20,400 saved vs $20,000 due. Covered, with $400 to spare.

If any row comes up short, you have three levers: save more per month, negotiate a later or split final payment with one vendor, or trim the budget before you sign the next contract. All three are much easier a year out than two weeks out.

Step 5: Keep a buffer for the extras

The contract prices above are rarely the whole story. Tips for vendor teams, alteration fees, last-minute rentals, hair and makeup trials and the marriage licence all tend to show up late. A common guideline is to hold back roughly 5–10% of the total as a contingency line. On a $20,000 wedding that would be $1,000–$2,000. Our example couple's $400 cushion is thin, so they might raise their monthly saving or cut one line to build a bigger buffer.

Step 6: Track payments as you make them

Once the schedule is set, update it every time money goes out. For each payment, record the date paid, the amount, the method and a receipt or confirmation number. This protects you if a vendor's records ever disagree with yours, and it shows at a glance what's still owed.

Tip: put every due date in a shared calendar with a reminder one week ahead. Late payments can sometimes put a booking at risk, and a reminder costs nothing.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Budgeting the total but not the timing. The two-week crunch is the most common surprise.
  • Forgetting that some balances move. Catering and bar bills often depend on final guest numbers, so update the schedule when your headcount changes.
  • Paying everything by card without a plan to clear it. Card rewards can be useful, but only if the money to pay the statement is already saved.
  • Skipping the contract details. Read the cancellation and refund terms before paying a deposit, not after.

This guide is general information, not financial or legal advice. Always follow the terms in your own contracts.
