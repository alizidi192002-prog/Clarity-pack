Sinking Funds Explained: How to Save for Big Bills Before They Arrive

Updated 2026-10-05 · 7 min read

A sinking fund is money you set aside a little at a time for a cost you already know is coming. Car insurance, Christmas, a vacation, new tires: none of these are surprises, yet they wreck budgets every year because they arrive as one large bill. Sinking funds turn each of them into a small monthly line.

What is a sinking fund?

It is a savings pot with a name, a target amount and a due date. You divide the target by the number of months you have, save that amount every month, and spend the pot when the bill arrives. Then you start filling it again.

The idea is simple, but it changes how a budget feels. Without sinking funds, a $720 insurance bill in March means a tight month, a credit card balance, or a raid on your savings. With one, March looks like every other month, because you have been paying $60 toward that bill since the previous April.

Sinking fund vs emergency fund

People often mix these up, and mixing them is how an emergency fund quietly disappears.

Sinking fundEmergency fund
What it coversCosts you can predictCosts you cannot predict
ExamplesInsurance, gifts, vacation, car serviceJob loss, urgent medical bill, broken boiler
Is it meant to be spent?Yes, on scheduleOnly when something goes wrong
How muchThe exact cost of the billOften a few months of essential expenses

A quick test: if you can put a rough date and a rough amount on it, it belongs in a sinking fund. Christmas falls on the same date every year, so it is not an emergency. If you are still building your safety net, read how to build an emergency fund first; a small starter cushion and one or two sinking funds can grow side by side.

Which sinking funds do you need?

Look back through twelve months of bank and card statements and mark every payment that was not monthly. Most households end up with a list drawn from these groups:

  • Annual and semi-annual bills: car and home insurance, property tax, yearly subscriptions, memberships, professional fees.
  • Car: servicing, tires, registration, repairs.
  • Home: appliance replacement, small repairs, furniture.
  • Seasonal spending: Christmas and other holidays, birthdays, back to school, vacations.
  • Health and pets: dental work, glasses, vet visits.
  • Planned purchases: a new phone or laptop, a wedding you are attending, a course.

You do not need all of them. Start with the three or four that hurt most last year. Five to eight funds is plenty for most people; beyond that the tracking becomes a chore and you stop doing it.

How to calculate each sinking fund

The formula is the same for every fund:

Monthly amount = (target amount − what you have already saved) ÷ months until the bill is due

For costs without a fixed price, such as car repairs, use last year's total as your target and adjust once you have a few months of real numbers.

Worked example: a full year of sinking funds

Here is a sample household that listed seven irregular costs for the year and spread each one over 12 months.

Sinking fundYearly costPer month
Car insurance$720$60
Car maintenance & repairs$600$50
Christmas & gifts$600$50
Vacation$1,200$100
Annual subscriptions$180$15
Home & appliance repairs$480$40
Vet & dental$300$25
Total$4,080$340

$4,080 ÷ 12 = $340 a month. That number is the real cost of this household's "unexpected" bills. It was always there; it just used to show up in lumps.

Worked example: starting late

You rarely start exactly twelve months before a bill. If you begin in October, the months left are shorter and the first round costs more. Take three of the funds above:

Sinking fundTargetMonths leftPer month nowPer month after

Car insurance (March)$7206$120$60
Vacation (July)$1,20010$120$100
Total$2,520$440$210

The catch-up phase costs $440 a month, against $210 once each fund has completed its first cycle. If $440 does not fit, lower a target rather than give up: a $450 Christmas over 3 months is $150 a month, which is still far better than putting the whole amount on a card in December.

Where to keep the money

You have three workable options. Pick the one you will actually maintain.

  • One savings account plus a tracker. All sinking fund money sits in a single account, and a spreadsheet records how much belongs to each fund. This is the simplest setup and it works with any bank.
  • Separate accounts or sub-accounts. Some banks let you open several named pots. The balance of each pot is the fund, so there is nothing to reconcile.
  • Cash envelopes. Useful for smaller funds such as gifts, less practical for a $720 insurance bill.

Whichever you choose, keep sinking funds out of your everyday checking account. Money that sits next to your grocery money gets spent on groceries.

How to fit sinking funds into your budget

  1. Treat the total as a bill. Put one line in your budget called "sinking funds" for the monthly total ($340 in the example) and pay it right after payday, ideally with an automatic transfer.
  2. Split it by paycheck if that is easier. Paid every two weeks? $340 a month is about $157 per paycheck across 26 paychecks ($4,080 ÷ 26 = $156.92). The paycheck budget calculator can help you see what is left after it.
  3. Record every withdrawal. When the insurance bill arrives, pay it from the fund and note it in your tracker so the balance per fund stays accurate.
  4. Review targets once a year. Premiums rise and plans change. A good time is when you set up your budget for the new year.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Too many funds at once. Twenty tiny pots are hard to follow. Combine small ones into a general "irregular bills" fund.
  • Borrowing between funds without a note. Moving $100 from vacation to car repairs is fine, but write it down, or July arrives with a gap you forgot about.
  • Using the emergency fund for predictable costs. If you dip into it every December, you need a Christmas fund, not a bigger emergency fund.
  • Stopping after the bill is paid. The fund restarts the month after you spend it. That is what keeps the monthly amount low.
  • Saving into sinking funds while expensive debt grows. If you carry high-interest card debt, a common approach is to keep sinking funds for unavoidable bills only (insurance, car, essential repairs) and send the rest toward the debt.
Tip: start with the next bill on the calendar. One fund that works gives you a reason to add the second. A complete system you never set up helps nobody.
