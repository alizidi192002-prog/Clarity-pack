Holiday Dinner Budget: How to Host for 10 Without Overspending

Updated 2026-10-15 · 7 min read

Hosting a big holiday meal is one of those costs that feels small item by item and large at the checkout. A turkey here, a few bottles there, "just one more" side dish. The fix is simple: decide the total first, price the menu on paper, and only then go shopping.

1. Pick a total, then turn it into a per-guest number

Start with what you can comfortably spend without touching your rent or bills money. Then divide by the number of people at the table. That per-guest figure is the most useful number in this whole plan, because it makes every decision easy to check.

For example, $200 for 10 people is $20 per guest. If someone adds two more guests, you know right away the dinner needs another $40, or the menu needs to stretch.

Don't forget the people in your own household when you count heads. They eat too.

2. Write the menu before you write the shopping list

Most overspending happens because the menu grows while you shop. Write the full menu at home first: one main, three or four sides, bread, one or two starters, dessert and drinks. Then go through each dish and list what you need to buy, crossing off anything already in the cupboard.

A good guideline is to plan fewer dishes and make enough of each. Ten guests rarely need eight sides; four well-sized ones usually leave plenty of leftovers.

3. Price every line (a worked example)

Use your usual store's website or last receipt to put a price next to each item. Here is an example budget for a dinner for 10, where the host cooks everything. Your prices will differ by store and region; the point is the method.

CategoryWhat's includedBudget
MainOne large turkey or roast$35
SidesPotatoes, stuffing, vegetables, cranberry sauce$40
BreadRolls and butter$8
StartersCheese, crackers, a veggie tray$25
DessertTwo pies$24
DrinksSoft drinks, juice, coffee and anything else you serve$40
Pantry restockSpices, oil, flour, stock$18
DisposablesFoil pans, napkins, takeaway boxes for leftovers$10
Total$200

$200 ÷ 10 guests = $20 per guest. Notice the two lines people forget: the pantry restock and the disposables. Together they are $28 here, which is more than the bread and dessert combined. Leave them out of the plan and the dinner "goes over budget" even though the food didn't.

4. Cut costs with a potluck, without making it awkward

Sharing the meal is the single biggest lever you have. Guests often ask "what can I bring?", so have a real answer ready. Give each person or family one specific dish rather than "bring anything", so you don't end up with four desserts and no vegetables.

Here is the same dinner when the host keeps the main, bread, drinks, pantry and disposables, and guests bring the sides, starters and dessert:

CategoryHost cooks allPotluck (host's share)
Main$35$35
Sides$40$0
Bread$8$8
Starters$25$0
Dessert$24$0
Drinks$40$40
Pantry restock$18$18
Disposables$10$10
Total for the host$200$111

The host's cost drops from $200 to $111, a saving of $89. The guests together cover $89 of sides, starters and dessert, which is a modest amount each when spread across several households.

5. Spread the spending over five weeks

Paying $200 in one trip the week of the holiday is what makes it hurt. Most of the list keeps well, so buy it early in small pieces. Set aside $40 a week for five weeks (5 × $40 = $200) and shop in this order:

  • Weeks 1–2: pantry items, disposables and shelf-stable drinks. Nothing here spoils.
  • Weeks 3–4: the main if you buy it frozen, plus anything for the freezer such as rolls or pie crusts.
  • Week 5: fresh produce, dairy and cheese only. This should be the smallest trip.

Keep the receipts in one envelope or note the amounts in one list. After each trip, subtract from the total so you always know what's left.

6. Watch the small things that add up

  • Last-minute runs. One forgotten item often turns into a basket of extras. Check the list against the menu twice before the final trip.
  • Decorations and new serving dishes. These aren't food, so give them their own small line in your holiday budget or skip them this year.
  • Cooking too much. Leftovers are great for a day or two. Beyond that, extra food is just money in the bin. Send guests home with containers.
  • Drinks. This line can grow fast. Decide in advance what you will serve and how much, and let guests know if they're welcome to bring their own extras.

7. Fit the dinner into your full holiday budget

The dinner is only one part of December. Gifts, travel, cards and events all draw from the same money. Put the dinner in as its own line next to the others so you can see the full picture. If the total is too high, you can choose where to trim, for example a smaller gift budget or a potluck dinner, instead of finding out in January.

Quick checklist: total budget chosen → per-guest number worked out → menu written → every line priced → potluck dishes assigned → weekly set-aside started → receipts tracked in one place.
