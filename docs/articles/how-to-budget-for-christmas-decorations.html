How to Budget for Christmas Decorations: A Simple Plan (With Example)

Updated 2026-11-22 · 6 min read

Decorations are the holiday cost nobody plans for. Gifts get a list and food gets a menu, but the tree, the lights and "just one more" ornament usually go on the card in small, unplanned pieces. A short decorations budget keeps the house festive without eating into the gift money.

1. Give decorations their own line

The first step is simply to separate decorations from everything else. If they share a single "Christmas" pot with gifts and food, they tend to get bought first (in late November, when the shops fill up) and gifts get squeezed later. Write three lines in your holiday budget: gifts, food and hosting, decorations. Each gets its own number.

How big should the decorations line be? There is no fixed rule. A useful guideline is to keep it small compared with gifts, because most decorations come back out of the box every year. If you are setting your gift total at the same time, the free Christmas gift budget calculator helps you size that part first, so decorations fit around it rather than the other way round.

2. Take stock before you shop

Open last year's boxes before you go anywhere near a store. Plug in every strand of lights and check the bulbs. Count ornaments, hooks, extension leads and timers. Make two lists:

  • Works and we'll use it: this costs nothing this year.
  • Broken, missing or worn out: these are your only "must replace" items.

This 20-minute check stops the most common decoration waste: buying lights or ornaments you already own because you couldn't find them, or because you assumed last year's strand had died.

3. Split spending into "must" and "nice to have"

Go room by room and write down what you want, with a realistic price next to each item. Then mark each one:

  • Must: the tree (if you buy a real one each year), replacement lights, anything broken that you use every year.
  • Nice to have: a new theme, extra outdoor pieces, a second wreath, new stockings.

Fund the musts first. Only add nice-to-haves while the total stays under your limit. If an item doesn't fit this year, put it on next year's list; it may also be cheaper after the holidays (see step 6).

4. A worked example: a $150 decorations budget

Here is an example for a household that already owns most of its decorations and buys a real tree each year. The prices are illustrative; use what things cost where you live.

ItemTypeAmount
Real tree (about 6 ft)Must$65
Replace 2 strands of lightsMust$20
Front door wreathNice to have$25
Ornament top-upNice to have$15
Table and mantel (greenery, candles)Nice to have$15
Storage boxes and labelsMust$10
Outdoor display (reusing last year's)—$0
Total$150

The musts come to $65 + $20 + $10 = $95. That leaves $55 for nice-to-haves, and the wreath, ornaments and table pieces add up to exactly $25 + $15 + $15 = $55. If the tree turns out to cost $80 instead of $65, the plan already tells you what gives: drop the ornament top-up and you are back at $150.

5. Real or artificial tree? Compare the cost per year

The tree is usually the biggest single decoration cost, so it's worth doing the maths once. A real tree is a yearly cost. An artificial tree costs more up front, but you spread that over every year you use it. Using example prices:

OptionUp-front costYears usedCost per year
Real tree, bought every year$651$65
Artificial tree$2406$40

Over those six years, the real trees cost $65 × 6 = $390 and the artificial tree costs $240, a difference of $150. But the artificial tree only wins if you really keep it for years, and you need somewhere to store it. If you'd use it for three years, it works out at $80 a year, more than the real tree. Many people simply prefer a real tree, and that's a fair choice; the point is to know what it costs so you can budget for it.

6. Use after-Christmas sales the right way

Decorations are often discounted once the holiday is over. That can be a good time to buy, but only for things already on your list. A simple approach:

  • Keep the "next year" list from step 3 on your phone.
  • Set a small post-holiday limit, for example $20 or $30, and take it from January's budget, not December's.
  • Buy only items on the list. A cheap ornament you didn't plan for is still unplanned spending.

7. Lower-cost ways to decorate

  • Bring in nature: pine cones, branches and greenery from your own garden (or offcuts some tree sellers give away) make good table and mantel pieces.
  • Swap with friends: if someone is changing their colour theme, their old ornaments may suit you.
  • Use a timer for lights: it doesn't cost much, and lights left on all night add to the electricity bill. If winter bills worry you, see how to budget for winter heating bills.
  • Make a few things: paper stars, dried orange slices or a homemade wreath can be a fun family evening that costs very little.
  • Choose one focus area: a well-decorated tree and front door often look better than small touches spread thinly across every room.

8. Pack up for next year (and save money doing it)

How you put decorations away decides what you'll have to rebuy. Wrap lights around a piece of card so they don't tangle and break. Store fragile ornaments in egg boxes or divided boxes. Label each box by room. Put a note in the top box listing anything that broke this year, so next November's "take stock" step takes five minutes instead of twenty.

Finally, write down what you actually spent next to your plan. Next year you'll start from a real number instead of a guess.

Tip: buy the tree and replacement lights first and write down the real prices straight away. Once the musts are paid for, you know exactly how much is left for the fun extras.
