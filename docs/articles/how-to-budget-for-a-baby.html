How to Budget for a Baby: Startup Costs, Monthly Costs and a Savings Plan

Updated 2026-11-15 · 7 min read

A baby changes your budget in three ways at once: a pile of one-time purchases before the birth, a new set of monthly costs after it, and often a drop in income while one parent is on leave. Plan for all three separately and the numbers become much less scary.

The figures below are an example for one household, not averages. Prices depend a lot on where you live, what you buy new or second-hand, and how you feed your baby, so replace every number with your own quotes.

1. List the one-time startup costs

Start with the things you need before the baby comes home. Keep the list short: babies need far less than shops suggest, and friends and family often give clothes and small items. Write a price next to each item after checking two or three shops or second-hand listings.

Startup itemExample cost
Crib and mattress$250
Car seat (buy new so you know its history)$180
Stroller$220
Clothes for the first 3 months$120
Bottles and feeding supplies$80
Changing and bath supplies$60
Baby monitor$70
Total startup costs$980

Two ways to bring this total down: buy large items second-hand where it is safe to do so (strollers and cribs that meet current safety standards are often easy to find), and wait until after the birth for "nice to have" items. Many parents find they never use some of them.

2. Estimate the new monthly costs

Next, list the costs that will repeat every month. These are the ones that change your budget for years, so they matter more than the startup list.

Monthly costExample cost
Diapers and wipes$80
Formula (skip or reduce if breastfeeding)$150
Clothes as the baby grows$30
Toiletries and basic medicine$25
Extra laundry, heating and water$20
Total new monthly costs$305

Childcare is not in this table on purpose. If you will use daycare or a childminder, it is often the biggest cost of all and can be larger than everything above combined. Get real quotes from local providers early, because waiting lists are common, and treat it as its own line in your budget.

Also check what your health insurance covers for the birth and for baby check-ups, and add any expected out-of-pocket costs to the startup list.

3. Plan for lower income during leave

Parental leave rules and pay vary a lot by country and employer. Ask your HR team or check your local government rules for exactly how many weeks are paid and at what rate. Then work out the gap:

Monthly income gap × months of reduced pay = total leave gap.

For example, if take-home pay drops by $1,200 a month for 3 months, the gap is $1,200 × 3 = $3,600. That money has to come from savings set aside before the birth, or from cutting other spending during those months. Seeing the number early gives you time to prepare.

4. Build a baby fund before the due date

Combine the startup costs with a small cushion for the first few months, when you are still learning what you really spend:

Baby fund partAmount
Startup costs (step 1)$980
3 months of new monthly costs ($305 × 3)$915
Baby fund target$1,895

With 6 months to go, that is $1,895 ÷ 6 ≈ $316 a month. Keep it in a separate savings account, like a sinking fund, so it doesn't get spent on other things. If you also have a leave gap from step 3, save for it on top of this, or keep your emergency fund untouched for true emergencies.

5. Rebuild your monthly budget

Once the baby arrives, the new costs need a permanent place in your budget. Here is the same household before and after, with a take-home pay of $4,200 a month:

CategoryBefore babyAfter baby
Housing$1,400$1,400
Food and groceries$600$550
Transport$350$350
Bills and utilities$300$300
Fun and eating out$400$200
Baby costs (step 2)$0$305
Savings$650$595
Other$500$500
Total$4,200$4,200

The $305 of new baby costs came from three places: $200 less on fun and eating out (which often happens naturally with a newborn), $50 less on groceries, and $55 less going to savings. Every household will find the money in different places. The point is to decide where it comes from on purpose, instead of letting the card balance decide for you.

If your pay comes in every two weeks or every week, the free paycheck budget calculator helps you split these monthly amounts across each paycheck.

6. Review after the first three months

Your first estimates will be wrong in some places, and that is normal. After three months, compare what you planned with what you actually spent. Diapers, formula and laundry are often different from what people expect. Adjust the baby line, then look ahead: the costs change again as the baby starts solid food, needs bigger clothes, or starts childcare.

Tip: keep a simple "wait list" for baby purchases. When you feel you need something, write it down and wait a week. Many items stop feeling necessary, and the ones that remain are the ones you'll actually use.

This guide is general information, not financial advice. Check leave rules, insurance cover and childcare prices for your own situation.
