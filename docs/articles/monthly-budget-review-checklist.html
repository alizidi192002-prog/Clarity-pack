Monthly Budget Review Checklist: 8 Steps in 20 Minutes

Updated 2026-10-27 · 6 min read

Making a budget is the easy part. What makes it work is a short review at the end of every month: what you planned, what really happened, and what you'll change. Here is a checklist you can run in about 20 minutes, with a full worked example.

Why a monthly review matters more than the budget itself

A budget is a guess about the future. The first month is almost always wrong somewhere: groceries cost more than you thought, a bill was higher, a forgotten subscription renewed. Without a review, those small misses repeat every month and you quietly stop trusting the plan. With a review, each month's budget gets a little more accurate, and after three or four months it reflects how you really live.

Pick a fixed time, for example the first Sunday of the month or the day after payday, and put it in your calendar like an appointment. Short and regular beats long and rare.

The checklist

1. Gather the numbers

Download or open last month's bank and card statements. Make sure every transaction is in your budget sheet or app, including cash withdrawals and small card payments. If you share costs with a partner, include their spending on shared categories too.

2. Check income: did you get what you expected?

Compare the take-home pay you planned with what actually arrived. Note any extra money (a refund, overtime, a side job) and any shortfall. Extra money should get a job straight away: a goal, a sinking fund or savings, rather than sitting in your current account.

3. Compare budget vs actual for every category

Put planned and actual side by side and calculate the difference. Under budget is a positive number, over budget is negative. This is the heart of the review.

4. Mark the categories that went over

For each overspent category, ask one question: was this a one-off, or is the budget simply too low? A car repair is a one-off. Groceries over budget three months in a row means the grocery number is unrealistic.

5. Look for leaks

Scan the transactions for subscriptions you don't use, bank fees, late fees or duplicate charges. These are the easiest savings you'll ever find, because cancelling them costs you nothing in daily life.

6. Check savings and goals

Did the planned transfers to savings, your emergency fund and your sinking funds actually happen? Update the balance of each goal. Seeing progress written down is what keeps the habit alive.

7. Calculate your savings rate

Savings rate = money saved ÷ take-home income. It's the single number that tells you whether the month went well overall. Track it month by month rather than judging any one month on its own.

8. Adjust next month's budget

Move money between categories so the plan matches reality. The important rule: if you raise one category, lower another by the same amount, so the total still fits your income. Then look ahead: is there a birthday, renewal or holiday next month that needs extra room?

Worked example: an October review

Sam takes home $3,100 a month and planned to spend $3,000, keeping $100 as a buffer. Here's the October review:

CategoryPlannedActualDifference
Housing$1,200$1,200$0
Groceries$450$512−$62
Transport$220$185+$35
Bills & utilities$260$274−$14
Eating out & fun$200$268−$68
Sinking funds$205$205$0
Savings transfer$300$300$0
Other$165$106+$59
Total$3,000$3,050−$50

What the review shows:

  • Overall: Sam spent $50 more than planned, so the $100 buffer shrank to $50. Not a disaster, but worth understanding.
  • Groceries (−$62): the third month in a row over budget. The $450 figure is too low, not Sam's shopping.
  • Eating out & fun (−$68): two birthday dinners. A one-off, but birthdays happen every year, so next year they belong in a sinking fund.
  • Transport and Other (+$35 and +$59): both came in under budget, which leaves room to rebalance.
  • Savings rate: Sam moves the leftover $50 into savings, so the month's total saved is $300 + $50 = $350. $350 ÷ $3,100 ≈ 11.3%.

Adjusting November's budget

Sam raises groceries to a realistic number and pays for it from the categories that came in under budget. The total stays at $3,000:

CategoryOctober planChangeNovember plan
Groceries$450+$50$500
Transport$220−$20$200
Other$165−$30$135
All other categories$2,165$0$2,165
Total$3,000$0$3,000

Notice what Sam did not do: cut "eating out & fun" to zero as punishment. Budgets that feel like punishment get abandoned. The overspend had a clear reason, so the category stays at $200.

Common mistakes in a monthly review

  • Skipping it after a bad month. The months that went wrong are the ones that teach you the most. Review them first.
  • Only looking at the total. A month can look fine overall while one category is over every single time. Check each line.
  • Changing too much at once. Adjust two or three categories per month. Big rewrites make it hard to see what worked.
  • Forgetting next month's irregular costs. November and December carry holidays, gifts and often higher heating bills. Plan them before the month starts. Our guide on sinking funds shows how to spread those costs over the year.
  • Not writing the result down. Keep a small log of your monthly savings rate. After a year you'll see the trend clearly, which is far more useful than any single month.
Quick version: if you only have five minutes, check three things: total spent vs planned, the one category that went over the most, and how much you saved. Then fix that one category for next month.
