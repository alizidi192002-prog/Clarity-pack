How to Track Subscriptions and Cut Your Monthly Bills (Step by Step)

Updated 2026-11-03 · 7 min read

Subscriptions are easy to start and easy to forget. A few dollars here and a free trial there add up, and because they charge automatically, you rarely see the total. One focused hour is usually enough to find them all, decide what stays, and lower a couple of fixed bills too.

1. Make a complete list (look in four places)

Most people can name their main streaming service and their phone plan. The forgotten charges are the ones that matter here. Check these four places and write down every recurring payment you find:

  • Bank and card statements: go back at least three months, so you catch anything billed every two or three months. Search for words like "subscription", "membership", "renewal" or "monthly".
  • Annual renewals: scroll back 12 months for once-a-year charges such as software, cloud storage, domain names or memberships. These are the easiest to miss.
  • App stores: your phone's app store account has a subscriptions page that lists everything billed through it.
  • Your email inbox: search for "receipt", "your plan", "trial" and "renews". This also finds services billed to an old card or a partner's card.

For each item write the name, the amount, how often it charges, the date it renews, and which card it's on. That last detail saves time later when you cancel or switch.

2. Turn every charge into a monthly and a yearly number

Different billing cycles hide the real cost. Convert everything to the same unit: divide yearly charges by 12, and multiply weekly charges by 52 then divide by 12. Then look at the yearly total. $9.99 a month feels small; $119.88 a year is something you might think twice about buying in one go.

3. Sort each one: keep, cancel or downgrade

Go down the list and ask one question per line: "If this wasn't already running, would I sign up for it today at this price?" Then put it in one of three groups:

  • Keep — you use it every week and it's worth the price to you.
  • Cancel — you haven't used it in the last month, or another service does the same job.
  • Downgrade — you use it, but a cheaper tier, an ad-supported plan, or a shared family plan would do.

4. Cancel properly (and stop free trials from converting)

Cancel on the same day you decide, while it's fresh. A few habits help:

  • Cancel through the service's account page or app store, not by deleting the app. Deleting an app doesn't stop billing.
  • Take a screenshot of the cancellation confirmation, in case you're charged again.
  • For every new free trial, put a reminder in your calendar two days before it ends.
  • Rotate instead of stacking: keep one video service at a time and switch when you've watched what you wanted.

5. Lower your fixed bills

Subscriptions are only half of the recurring money. Phone, internet and insurance usually cost more, and the prices often creep up after an introductory deal ends. Once a year, for each bill:

  1. Check what you're paying now and what you actually use (data, internet speed, coverage).
  2. Look up what the same provider and two competitors charge new customers for a similar plan.
  3. Call or chat with your provider, say you're comparing offers, and ask if a cheaper plan or loyalty discount is available. If not, consider switching.

The same household might end up with something like this:

That's $44 a month, or $528 a year. Combined with the cancelled subscriptions, the household keeps $66.98 a month ($803.76 a year). These figures are only an illustration: what you can save depends on your providers and your area. Before raising an insurance deductible, make sure you could pay it from savings if you had to claim.

6. Send the savings somewhere on purpose

Money freed from cancelled bills tends to disappear into everyday spending unless you give it a job. Pick one and set up an automatic transfer for the same amount on payday:

  • Your emergency fund, if it isn't built yet (see how to build an emergency fund).
  • Extra payments on a debt Extra payments on a debt — the debt snowball vs avalanche calculator shows how much faster it could be paid off.mdash; see debt snowball vs avalanche to choose which one to pay first.
  • A sinking fund for a planned expense such as the holidays or a car repair.

Keep it from creeping back

Subscriptions return quietly, so make the audit a habit rather than a one-off:

  • Monthly (5 minutes): scan your statement for any new recurring charge during your normal budget review.
  • Before each renewal: look at your list for annual charges coming up in the next 30 days and decide before they bill.
  • Once a year: repeat step 5 for phone, internet and insurance.

A simple guideline many people use: keep all subscriptions together under a set limit, for example a fixed amount per month you choose in your budget. If you want to add a new one, something else has to go.

Tip: put all subscriptions on one card if you can. One statement to check makes the monthly scan take a couple of minutes instead of twenty.
