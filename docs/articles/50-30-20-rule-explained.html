The 50/30/20 Rule Explained: How to Split Your Paycheck (With a Worked Example)

Updated 2026-10-06 · 8 min read

The 50/30/20 rule is the simplest budget there is: half of your take-home pay goes to needs, 30% to wants, and 20% to savings and extra debt payments. Three buckets instead of thirty categories. Here is how it works, where people get stuck, and what to do when your real numbers don't fit.

What the 50/30/20 rule is

The rule is a guideline for dividing your after-tax income into three parts:

  • 50% needs — the bills you must pay to live and work.
  • 30% wants — everything you choose to spend on because you enjoy it.
  • 20% savings and debt — money that makes your future self better off.

It was popularised by the book All Your Worth by Elizabeth Warren and Amelia Warren Tyagi. It is a starting point, not a law. The percentages are a benchmark to compare yourself against, and it is normal to adjust them to your rent, your city and your stage of life.

Step 1: Find your take-home pay

Use the money that actually lands in your bank account each month, after tax. If retirement contributions or health insurance are taken out of your paycheck before you see it, you can add them back and count them inside the right bucket (retirement in savings, insurance in needs). If that feels like too much work, just use the amount that hits your account. Being consistent matters more than being perfect.

Step 2: Know what goes in each bucket

Needs (50%)

Rent or mortgage, basic groceries, utilities, transport to work, insurance, childcare, and the minimum payment on every debt. A quick test: if you stopped paying it, would there be a serious consequence within a month or two? Then it is a need.

Wants (30%)

Restaurants and takeaway, streaming and other subscriptions, clothes beyond the basics, hobbies, trips, gifts, upgrades. Wants are not bad. This bucket is the reason the budget is livable, and a budget with no fun in it rarely lasts past the second month.

Savings and debt (20%)

Emergency fund, retirement, savings goals such as a house deposit, and any debt payment above the minimum. The minimum is a need because you have no choice. The extra is a decision that builds your net worth, so it belongs here.

Grey areas: the same category can be split across two buckets. A basic phone plan is a need; the newest phone on a payment plan is a want. Groceries are a need; the weekly treats in the trolley are a want. Don't agonise over it. Pick a rule, write it down, and apply it the same way every month.

Step 4: Adjust the rule to fit your life

If your needs are above 50%, you have three honest options:

  1. Lower a need. The big ones move the needle: housing, transport, insurance. Shopping around for insurance or a cheaper phone plan can be done this month. Housing takes longer.
  2. Take the gap from wants. Trim wants and send the difference to savings.
  3. Use a different split for now. A ratio you can actually keep beats a perfect one you abandon.

Here is what three common splits look like on Maya's $3,600:

SplitNeedsWantsSavingsTotal
50/30/20$1,800$1,080$720$3,600
60/20/20$2,160$720$720$3,600
70/20/10$2,520$720$360$3,600

Maya chooses 60/20/20. Her needs limit becomes $2,160, so she only has to find $20 there (a cheaper phone plan covers it). She brings wants down from $900 to $720 by eating out less, and that frees up exactly the $200 she was missing in savings: $520 + $200 = $720, a full 20%. Same income, same flat, and she now saves $2,400 more over a year ($200 × 12).

Common mistakes

  • Using gross pay. The percentages apply to take-home pay. Using your salary before tax makes every bucket look bigger than it is.
  • Calling wants "needs". A gym membership, a second streaming service or a car upgrade feel essential, but they are choices. Be strict here or the needs bucket swallows everything.
  • Saving what is left over. There is rarely anything left. Move the savings amount on payday, by automatic transfer if you can, and spend what remains.
  • Forgetting irregular bills. Annual insurance, car repairs and holidays still count. Divide them by 12 and include the monthly amount in the right bucket.
  • Giving up because the numbers don't match. Almost nobody hits 50/30/20 exactly. The point is to see where you stand and move one step closer each month.

Is the 50/30/20 rule right for you?

It works well if you are new to budgeting, dislike tracking every purchase, or want a quick health check on your spending. It works less well if your income is very low or housing is very expensive where you live, because needs alone can pass 60 or 70%. In that case, use a split that fits and focus on keeping some savings habit alive, even a small one. If you have no cushion at all yet, start with our guide on how to build an emergency fund and send the first part of your 20% there.

Tip: review the split every three months, and any time your rent or pay changes. When you get a raise, send most of the extra to the savings bucket before your wants get used to it.
