How to Build an Emergency Fund: A Step-by-Step Plan From $0

Updated 2026-10-02 · 8 min read

An emergency fund is money set aside for the things you can't plan: a job loss, a car repair, a medical bill, a broken boiler. It doesn't need to be big to be useful. The first few hundred dollars already change how a bad week feels. Here is how to work out your number and reach it, even if you are starting from zero.

1. Decide what counts as an emergency

Write the rule down before you save a single dollar, because the hardest part of an emergency fund is not spending it. A simple test is three questions:

  • Is it unexpected? Christmas and car insurance are not surprises. They happen every year.
  • Is it necessary? A repair that gets you to work is. A sale on a new phone is not.
  • Is it urgent? If it can wait two months, you can save for it from your normal budget.

If the answer is yes to all three, use the fund without guilt. That is what it is for. Costs that are predictable but irregular belong in a separate pot, planned month by month. Our guide on making a budget for 2027 shows how to map those.

2. Work out your target from essential expenses

A common guideline is to hold three to six months of expenses. Note the word expenses, not income, and only the essential ones: what you would still have to pay if your income stopped tomorrow. Restaurants, holidays and streaming are things you would pause, so leave them out.

Here is a worked example for one household:

Essential expensePer month
Rent$1,100
Groceries$420
Transport$220
Utilities$180
Insurance$160
Minimum debt payments$150
Phone & internet$90
Total essentials$2,320

With essentials of $2,320 a month, three months is $6,960 and six months is $13,920. That range is your long-term target.

Three months or six?

Lean towards the lower end if your income is stable, your household has two earners, and you have few dependants. Lean towards the higher end if you are self-employed, paid on commission, the only earner, or work in a field where finding a new job takes a while. There is no single correct answer, so pick the number that would let you sleep.

3. Start with a small first milestone

$6,960 looks impossible when you have nothing saved, and big distant targets are easy to give up on. So split it into stages:

  • Stage 1: a starter buffer of $1,000 (or one month of rent, if that is easier to picture). This covers most everyday emergencies, like a repair or an urgent trip.
  • Stage 2: one full month of essentials, $2,320 in the example.
  • Stage 3: three months, then decide whether you need to go further.

Each stage is a finish line. Reaching the first one quickly matters more than the size of it.

4. Find the monthly amount

The money usually comes from several small changes rather than one big cut. Look at your last month of bank statements and aim for a mix like this:

Where it comes fromPer month
Automatic transfer on payday$120
Two more home-cooked dinners a week$80
Selling unused items or a few extra hours of work$60
Two cancelled subscriptions$30
Total saved$290

At $290 a month, the timeline for the example household looks like this:

MilestoneTargetMonths at $290
Starter buffer$1,0004
One month of essentials$2,3208
Three months of essentials$6,96024

Two years sounds long, but you are protected from month four onwards, and any windfall shortens it. A tax refund, a bonus or a third paycheck in a month can go straight into the fund. If you are not sure how much room your budget has for saving, the 50/30/20 calculator gives you a quick starting split.

5. Make it automatic and keep it separate

Two habits do most of the work:

  • Automate it. Set a standing transfer for the day after payday. Saving what is left at the end of the month rarely works, because there is rarely anything left.
  • Separate it. Keep the fund in its own savings account, not in the account your card is linked to. You want it easy to reach in a real emergency, within a day or two, but out of sight the rest of the time.

An emergency fund is for safety, not growth. It should be somewhere you can withdraw from without penalties and where the balance can't fall. A savings account that pays some interest is a bonus, not the goal.

6. Emergency fund or debt first?

If you have high-interest debt, such as credit cards, a widely used approach is to do both in order: build the starter buffer first, then put most of your spare money towards the debt, then come back and finish the full fund. The reason for the buffer is practical. Without it, the next surprise bill goes back on the card and undoes your progress.

Keep paying every minimum throughout. When you get to the debt stage, our comparison of the debt snowball and avalanche methods helps you choose the order.

7. Refill it after you use it

Using the fund is not a failure. It means the plan worked. Afterwards, write down what you took out and restart the transfers until the balance is back at your target. If the same "emergency" keeps appearing, like car repairs every few months, it is really a regular cost and deserves its own line in your budget.

Review the target once a year, or whenever your rent, family size or job changes. If your essentials rise from $2,320 to $2,500, your three-month target rises from $6,960 to $7,500.

Tip: give the account a name, like "Emergency only". It sounds small, but a label makes it much harder to dip into for a weekend away.

Quick recap

  • Add up your essential monthly expenses, not your full spending.
  • Aim for $1,000 first, then one month, then three to six months.
  • Automate a transfer the day after payday into a separate account.
  • Use it only for unexpected, necessary, urgent costs, and refill it afterwards.
