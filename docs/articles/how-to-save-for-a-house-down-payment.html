How to Save for a House Down Payment: A Step-by-Step Plan

Updated 2026-11-10 · 7 min read

A down payment is the biggest savings goal most people ever set, and that's exactly why it feels impossible. The trick is to stop staring at the big number and turn it into a monthly amount you can see in your budget. Here's how to do that, step by step.

1. Pick a realistic price range first

Your target depends entirely on the kind of home you plan to buy, so start there. Look at recent listings for the area and size you want and write down a price range, not a dream number. If you plan to buy with a partner, agree on the range together before you save a cent, because it changes everything that follows.

It also helps to talk to a lender or mortgage broker early. They can tell you roughly what you might be approved for and which loan types are open to you. Different loans ask for different minimum down payments, and the rules vary by country and lender, so get the details for your own situation instead of relying on a number you read online.

2. Decide on your down payment percentage

A common guideline is to put down 20% of the price, because in many places a smaller down payment means paying for mortgage insurance or getting a higher rate. But plenty of buyers put down 5% or 10% and accept that cost so they can buy sooner. There's no single right answer. A bigger down payment means a smaller loan and lower monthly payments; a smaller one means you buy sooner but borrow more.

Choose the percentage that fits your timeline, then write it down. You can always save more than the target, but you need a target to plan around.

3. Add the costs people forget

The down payment isn't the only cash you need on the day you buy. Closing costs (fees for the lender, legal work, inspections, taxes and insurance set-up) are paid upfront too, and they're often a few percent of the price. Then there's moving, basic furniture and the first small repairs. Ask your lender for an estimate of closing costs for your area and use that number.

Here's a worked example for a $300,000 home with a 10% down payment:

ItemAmount
Down payment (10% of $300,000)$30,000
Closing costs (estimate: 3% of price)$9,000
Moving, basic furniture, first repairs$3,000
Total cash needed$42,000
Already saved−$6,000
Still to save$36,000

The 3% here is only an example. Your own estimate could be higher or lower, so replace it with a real quote as soon as you have one.

4. Turn the total into a monthly number

Divide what's left by the number of months until you want to buy. This one division is what makes the goal real. Using the $36,000 from the example:

TimelineMonthsSave per month
2 years24$1,500
3 years36$1,000
4 years48$750

If the monthly number is far beyond what your budget can handle, you have three levers: a longer timeline, a cheaper home, or a smaller down payment percentage. Adjust one at a time until the number is hard but doable.

5. Find the money in your budget

Most people don't find $1,000 a month in one place. They build it from several smaller changes. Start by writing down your take-home pay and fixed bills, then look for lines you can trim. The free 50/30/20 calculator is a quick way to see how much of your income is going to wants versus savings right now.

Here's how the $1,000 a month from the example could be put together:

SourcePer month
Current monthly savings, redirected to the house fund$400
Eating out less$150
Cancelling unused subscriptions$50
Switching to a cheaper phone plan$40
Side income (weekend work or freelancing)$200
Half of raises, tax refunds and gifts (averaged)$160
Total$1,000

The last line matters more than it looks. Deciding in advance that half of every raise or windfall goes to the house fund stops that money from quietly disappearing into everyday spending.

6. Automate it and keep it separate

Open a separate savings account just for the house and name it something like "House fund". Set an automatic transfer for the day after payday, so the money moves before you can spend it. Keeping it apart from your emergency fund is important: if the car breaks down, you don't want to raid the down payment.

Because you'll need this money on a specific date, most people keep it somewhere safe and easy to access, such as a savings account, rather than in investments that can drop in value right before you buy. What suits you depends on your timeline and situation, so check the options available where you live.

7. Protect the plan

  • Keep an emergency fund too. A few months of essential expenses in a separate account means a surprise bill doesn't set the house fund back. Our guide on how to build an emergency fund walks through it.
  • Avoid new debt. A new car loan or a large credit card balance can make it harder to get approved and lowers what you can borrow.
  • Track progress monthly. Write the balance down once a month. Watching it grow is the best motivation you'll get on a multi-year goal.
  • Review the target every six months. Prices, rates and your plans change. Update the price range and closing cost estimate, then recalculate the monthly number.
Tip: if you can't hit the full monthly amount yet, start with whatever you can and raise the transfer by a small amount every few months. Starting small now beats waiting for the "right" time.

This guide is general information, not financial advice. For decisions about loans and how much to borrow, talk to a qualified lender or adviser.
