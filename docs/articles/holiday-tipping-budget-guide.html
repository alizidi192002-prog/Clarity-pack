Holiday Tipping Budget: Who to Tip and How Much to Set Aside

Updated 2026-11-16 · 6 min read

Holiday tips are the gift budget nobody writes down. A little for the hairstylist, an envelope for the cleaner, a card for the babysitter, and suddenly December costs a few hundred dollars more than planned. Put the tips on paper in November and they become a normal line in your holiday budget instead of a surprise.

Why holiday tips deserve their own budget line

Most people plan gifts for family and friends, but year-end tips go to a different group: the people who help you every week or month. Because each tip feels small, they rarely get planned. Added together, they can be as big as one or two family gifts.

Giving them their own line does two things. First, you see the real total before you commit to anything. Second, you can decide on purpose who gets what, instead of handing out cash at the door and hoping the account balance holds.

Step 1: List everyone who helps you during the year

Go through a normal month and write down each person who provides a regular service. Common examples:

  • Housekeeper or cleaner
  • Babysitter, nanny or childcare staff
  • Hairstylist, barber or nail technician
  • Dog walker or pet sitter
  • Building staff such as a doorman or superintendent
  • Mail carrier, package delivery driver, trash and recycling collectors
  • Personal trainer or regular tutor

Only list people you actually see regularly. Someone you used once in March doesn't need a year-end tip.

Step 2: Use simple guidelines to set amounts

There's no fixed rule, and amounts vary a lot by city and household. A common guideline people use is:

  • Regular paid helpers (cleaner, babysitter, dog walker): about one visit's or one week's pay.
  • Personal services (hairstylist, barber): about the cost of one appointment.
  • Building staff and collectors: a modest fixed amount per person, often shared with neighbors.
  • Teachers and childcare staff: often a small gift or gift card rather than cash; many schools have their own policies.

Some workers, especially government employees such as postal carriers in the US, have limits on the value of gifts they can accept and may not be allowed to take cash. Check the rules before you choose cash, and pick a small gift or a card if cash isn't allowed.

Treat these numbers as a starting point, not an obligation. A warm handwritten note with a smaller amount is far better than a big tip that ends up on a credit card.

Step 3: Build your tipping table

Here's an example for one household. The amounts follow the guidelines above: the cleaner comes every two weeks and is paid $80 a visit, a haircut costs $40, the babysitter usually earns $60 for an evening.

PersonHow it was setAmount
CleanerOne visit's pay$80
BabysitterOne evening's pay$60
Building superintendentFixed amount$50
Two childcare teachers$25 gift card each$50
HairstylistOne haircut$40
Dog walkerAbout one week's walks$40
Trash collectorsShared card$30
Mail carrierSmall gift (no cash)$20
Total$370

Check: 80 + 60 + 50 + 50 + 40 + 40 + 30 + 20 = $370.

Step 4: Make it fit, then save for it

Now compare the total with what you can afford. If $370 is too much, don't cut everyone a little. Decide who matters most and adjust from the bottom of the list. For example, this household could:

  • Replace the dog walker's cash with a $20 gift card and a note (saves $20).
  • Split the superintendent tip with a partner's gift budget, or give $30 instead of $50 (saves $20).

Those two changes bring the total from $370 to $330.

Once the number is set, spread it out. If you start in mid-November, you have about six weeks until the end of December. $370 ÷ 6 weeks is about $62 a week; $330 ÷ 6 is $55 a week. Move that amount into a separate holiday account or a labeled envelope each week, so the money is ready when you need it.

If you're also planning presents, add the tipping total to your gift budget and look at both together. Our free Christmas gift budget calculator helps you set a total for gifts first, so you can see what's left for tips.

Step 5: Hand out tips the easy way

  • Get cash early. Banks and ATMs get busy in late December. Withdraw what you need once and put each amount in a labeled envelope.
  • Use digital payment for people who prefer it. Many cleaners, sitters and dog walkers are happy with a payment app; add a short message.
  • Add a note. A sentence saying what you appreciate makes even a modest tip feel personal.
  • Tick it off. Mark each person as done in your list so you don't give twice or forget someone.

What to do when money is tight this year

Tipping is a kind gesture, not a bill. If your budget is stretched, it's fine to give smaller amounts, a homemade treat, or simply a thoughtful card. People who work in your home usually understand. What causes real stress is putting holiday extras on a card and paying interest on them into spring. If that's a worry, read how to avoid holiday debt before you plan the rest of December.

Tip: keep this year's list. Next November you'll already have names and amounts, and you can start saving a little each month from September so the holidays cost you nothing extra in December.

This guide is general information, not financial advice. Tipping customs vary by country, city and workplace rules.
