How to Build a Wedding Guest List That Fits Your Budget

Updated 2026-10-29 · 7 min read

The guest list is the biggest lever in a wedding budget. Every extra person adds a meal, a drink, a chair and an invitation, so the number of guests quietly decides how much everything else costs. Build the list with numbers first and feelings second, and the hard conversations get much easier.

1. Agree on the total budget before anyone writes a name

Start with the money, not the people. Sit down with your partner (and anyone contributing to the cost) and agree on one total figure you can pay without going into debt. Write it down. If you write the guest list first, it tends to grow to fit your hopes, and then the budget has to stretch to match it.

At this stage, also agree on who has a say in the list. If parents are paying for part of the wedding, they will often expect to invite some of their own friends. It is much easier to agree on a share up front ("each set of parents gets 15 spots") than to negotiate name by name later.

2. Split your costs into fixed and per-guest

Wedding costs fall into two groups:

  • Fixed costs stay the same whether you have 60 guests or 160: the venue hire fee, photographer, attire, officiant, music.
  • Per-guest costs grow with every person: catering, bar, rentals like chairs and linens, invitations and postage, favors.

Ask your caterer and venue for a per-head price, then add the smaller items to get one number: your cost per guest. Here is an example:

Per-guest itemCost per guest
Catering (meal and service)$68
Bar and drinks$17
Rentals (chair, linens, tableware)$6
Invitation, stationery and postage$4
Cost per guest$95

Check whether quotes include tax and service charges. A per-head price that leaves those out can make your cost per guest look lower than it really is.

3. Work out your maximum guest count

Now the simple formula: (total budget − fixed costs) ÷ cost per guest = maximum guests.

Say the couple in our example has a total budget of $24,000. Their fixed costs look like this:

Fixed costAmount
Venue hire fee$5,200
Photographer$2,800
Attire and alterations$1,500
Officiant and music$1,200
Total fixed costs$10,700

$24,000 − $10,700 = $13,300 left for guests. $13,300 ÷ $95 = 140 guests. That is the ceiling. It is a good idea to aim a little under it, because small extras (a cake-cutting fee, a vendor meal, an extra table) always appear late in planning.

Many couples also keep a small buffer for surprises; a common guideline is around 5–10% of the total, but choose what feels safe for you. If you want help splitting the rest of the budget, see our wedding budget breakdown by percentage.

4. Write the list in tiers

Instead of one long list, sort everyone into tiers. This turns "who do we cut?" into a clear decision about a whole group instead of a painful choice about one person.

  • Tier A – must invite: immediate family, grandparents, the wedding party.
  • Tier B – very close: close friends, aunts, uncles, first cousins.
  • Tier C – would love to have: wider friends, coworkers, family friends.
  • Tier D – extras: plus-ones for single guests who aren't in a couple, and children.

Here is how the example couple's first draft came out:

TierGuestsRunning totalCost at $95
A – must invite3838$3,610
B – very close5290$4,940
C – would love to have34124$3,230
D – plus-ones and kids26150$2,470
Total150$14,250

The guest costs come to $14,250, but only $13,300 is available. Adding the $10,700 of fixed costs gives $24,950, which is $950 over budget, or 10 guests too many.

5. Trim with clear rules, not one-off exceptions

The easiest cuts are rules you can apply to everyone, so no single person feels singled out. Some common ones:

  • Plus-ones only for couples: invite partners who are married, engaged or living together, but not new or casual dates.
  • Adults-only reception: or children of close family only.
  • Coworkers: invite only those you see outside work, or none.
  • The "last two years" test: if you haven't spoken in two years, they move down a tier.

Our example couple decided plus-ones would go only to established couples. That removed 10 names from Tier D, bringing the list to exactly 140 guests and the total to $10,700 + (140 × $95) = $13,300 + $10,700 = $24,000. On budget.

If you still need to cut more after applying rules, remove from the bottom tier first. And if you can't bear to cut, the other route is to lower the cost per guest: a buffet instead of a plated meal, a limited bar, or a lunch reception can all bring the per-head number down. Our guide on how to cut wedding costs has more ideas.

6. Plan for everyone you invite

It is tempting to invite more people than your maximum, expecting some to decline. That is a risk: in some years and some families, nearly everyone says yes. The safer approach is to budget for every person you invite. If you do want a backup list, keep it short and send those invitations only after the first wave of replies has come in, and early enough that the invite doesn't feel last-minute.

7. Track the list in one place

Keep a single spreadsheet with one row per guest: name, tier, side (yours, your partner's, parents'), address, invitation sent, RSVP, meal choice and any dietary needs. Add up the "yes" replies and multiply by your cost per guest every week or two. That way you always know your real guest cost, and you can give the caterer a confirmed number without a scramble at the end.

Tip: agree on the guest list before you book the venue. A venue that holds 200 makes a 140-person list feel small, and one that holds 120 forces cuts you didn't plan for. Your maximum guest count should choose the venue, not the other way round.

Wedding planning involves a lot of personal choices, and this guide is general information to help you organize the numbers, not financial advice.
