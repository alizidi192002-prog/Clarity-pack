Debt Snowball vs Avalanche: Which Payoff Method Should You Use?

Updated 2026-10-01 · 7 min read

If you have more than one debt, the hardest question isn't "should I pay it off?" but "which one first?". The debt snowball and the debt avalanche are the two most common answers. Both work. They just put your extra money in a different place, and that changes how fast you see progress and how much interest you pay.

The one rule both methods share

Before choosing a method, get this part right, because it's the same for both:

  1. Pay the minimum on every debt, every month. Missing a minimum usually means late fees and can hurt your credit, which wipes out any gains from a clever strategy.
  2. Decide on one fixed monthly amount for debt that's higher than the total of your minimums. The difference is your "extra" payment.
  3. Send all of the extra to one target debt until it's gone.
  4. When a debt is paid off, roll its payment into the next target. Your total monthly debt payment stays the same, but more of it goes to fewer debts. That rolling effect is what makes both methods speed up over time.

The only difference between snowball and avalanche is how you choose the target in step 3.

Debt snowball: smallest balance first

With the snowball, you list your debts from the smallest balance to the largest and ignore the interest rates. You attack the smallest one first. Once it's gone, its payment rolls into the next smallest, and so on.

The appeal is psychological. You clear a whole debt quickly, you have one less bill to think about, and that early win makes it easier to keep going. For many people, staying motivated is the real problem, not the maths.

Debt avalanche: highest interest rate first

With the avalanche, you list your debts from the highest interest rate to the lowest and ignore the balances. You attack the most expensive debt first.

Because the highest-rate debt is the one growing fastest, paying it down first means less money goes to interest overall. Mathematically, the avalanche always costs the same or less than the snowball. The catch: if your highest-rate debt is also a large one, it can take many months before you finish paying off your first debt.

A worked example with three debts

Let's say you have three debts and you can put $375 a month toward them in total.

Personal loan$8006%$40
Credit card B$1,50018%$45
Credit card A$3,00024%$90
Total$5,300$175

The minimums add up to $175, so you have $375 − $175 = $200 extra each month to send to your target debt.

Here is what happens with each method, assuming interest is charged monthly at the rates above, no new spending goes on the cards, and you keep paying $375 every month until everything is cleared:

SnowballAvalanche
Payoff orderLoan → Card B → Card ACard A → Card B → Loan
First debt fully paidMonth 4Month 12
All debt paidMonth 17Month 17
Total interest paid (approx.)$894$722

In this example the avalanche saves about $172 in interest ($894 − $722). Both methods finish in the same month, because the total you pay each month is the same. The snowball, though, gives you a fully paid-off debt in month 4 instead of month 12, which is eight months sooner.

Your own numbers will be different. The gap between the two methods gets bigger when your high-rate debts are large and your small debts have low rates, and it shrinks (or disappears) when your smallest debt also happens to have the highest rate. In that case both methods give the same order.

How to choose the right method for you

Choose the avalanche if…

  • You're motivated by numbers and saving money, even if the first win takes a while.
  • One of your debts has a much higher rate than the others (for example, a credit card next to a low-rate loan).
  • Your balances are large, so the interest difference adds up to a meaningful amount.

Choose the snowball if…

  • You've tried to pay off debt before and lost steam.
  • You have several small balances you could clear in a few months.
  • Having fewer bills and due dates to track would make your life simpler.

A useful way to think about it: the best method is the one you'll still be following a year from now. A plan that saves $172 on paper but gets abandoned in month five saves nothing.

A middle path

Some people start with the snowball to knock out one or two tiny balances for a quick win, then switch to the avalanche for the rest. There's no rule against mixing, as long as you keep paying every minimum and keep the total monthly payment fixed.

Five steps to start this week

  1. List every debt with its balance, interest rate and minimum payment. Your statements or online accounts will show all three.
  2. Add up the minimums. This is the floor you must pay each month.
  3. Pick your monthly debt amount. Look at your budget and find a number above the minimums you can keep paying every month. Even $50 extra makes a difference over time. The paycheck budget calculator can help you find room.
  4. Sort the list by balance (snowball) or by rate (avalanche), and mark your first target.
  5. Track it monthly. Update each balance once a month and cross off debts as they hit zero. Seeing the total drop is motivating with either method.

Mistakes that slow down either method

  • Adding new debt. If you keep using the cards you're paying off, no method can win. Consider removing saved card details from shopping sites while you pay down balances.
  • Lowering the payment after a debt is gone. The roll-over is the engine of both methods. When a debt is cleared, its payment goes to the next one, not back into everyday spending.
  • Having no cash buffer. Without a small emergency fund, a car repair goes straight back on a card. Many people keep a starter buffer (a few hundred dollars up to one month of expenses, as a rough guideline) before going all-in on extra payments.
  • Not checking for rate changes. Promotional 0% rates end. When one does, re-sort your list if you're using the avalanche.
