How to Budget for a Vacation: Price the Trip, Then Save for It Monthly

Updated 2026-11-23 · 6 min read

The vacations that cause money stress are usually the ones that were never priced. You book the flights, then the hotel, then spend freely once you arrive, and the card bill shows up a month after you're home. A vacation budget flips that order: decide the total first, save it month by month, and come home with nothing left to pay off.

1. Pick the trip and the date before anything else

You can't budget for "a holiday sometime next year". Write down three things: where you'd like to go, roughly how many nights, and which month. The month matters most, because it tells you how long you have to save. A trip in July 2027, planned now in late November, gives you seven months (December to June) to put money aside.

If you're torn between two destinations, price both using the steps below. Comparing two real totals is much easier than comparing two vague ideas.

2. Price every part of the trip

Break the vacation into categories and put a number on each. Look up actual prices for your dates where you can, rather than guessing. The main categories are:

  • Getting there: flights, train or fuel, plus airport parking or transfers and any baggage fees.
  • Where you sleep: price per night × number of nights, including cleaning fees and local taxes that are often added at checkout.
  • Food: a daily amount per day × number of days. Count breakfast, lunch, dinner, snacks and coffee.
  • Getting around: local transport, taxis, car hire and fuel at the destination.
  • Things to do: tours, tickets, entry fees, equipment rental.
  • Extras: souvenirs, travel insurance, a new swimsuit, pet sitting at home, phone roaming.

The "extras" line is the one people leave out, and it's often where a trip goes over.

3. Add a buffer

Prices change between planning and booking, and something unexpected nearly always comes up on the trip itself. A common guideline is to add around 10% of the total as a buffer. If you don't use it, it goes straight back into savings when you return.

Worked example: a 7-night trip for two

Here's a sample budget for two people taking a week-long summer trip. The numbers are an illustration; swap in real prices for your own destination.

That's $1,400 per person, and it's the number you save toward, not the price of the flights alone.

4. Divide by the months you have left

Take the total and divide it by the number of months until you need the money. In the example, the trip is in July and saving runs from December through June: $2,800 ÷ 7 months = $400 a month.

Keep in mind that some costs are paid long before you leave. Flights and accommodation deposits are often due when you book. If you plan to book in February, make sure the savings will cover those bookings by then, or book later once the fund has grown.

5. Find the monthly amount in your budget

If $400 a month doesn't fit, you have three levers: save for longer, trim the trip, or free up money elsewhere. Trimming is often painless. In the example, two fewer nights saves $260 on accommodation and $140 on food, which takes the total down by $400 before the buffer.

To see where the money could come from, run your take-home pay through the free 50/30/20 calculator. A vacation fund usually sits in the "wants" share, so a few smaller wants, like takeaway meals or an unused subscription, can make room for it.

6. Keep the vacation money separate

Move the monthly amount into a separate savings account or a clearly labelled pot on payday, before you spend on anything else. Money that sits in your everyday account tends to get used. This is the same idea as a sinking fund; our guide to sinking funds explains how to run several of these at once.

7. Track spending while you're away

Your budget doesn't stop when the trip starts. A simple daily limit works well: take the food, transport, activities and extras lines and divide by the number of days. In the example, that's ($490 + $150 + $260 + $100) ÷ 7 = about $143 a day for two. Check it each evening. A big day can be balanced by a quieter one, and you'll know exactly where you stand.

When you get home, write down what each category actually cost. That gives you real numbers for the next trip, and any unused buffer can go back into savings.

Tip: put the trip into your yearly plan now. If you're setting up your budget for 2027, add the vacation as a fixed monthly line alongside other big irregular costs, so it's treated like a bill rather than an afterthought.
