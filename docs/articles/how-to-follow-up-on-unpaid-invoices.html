How to Follow Up on Unpaid Invoices: A Polite, Step-by-Step Schedule

Updated 2026-10-21 · 6 min read

Most late invoices aren't a sign of a bad client. They're usually stuck in an inbox, waiting on an approval, or missing a detail the accounts team needs. A calm, regular follow-up schedule gets most of them paid without awkward conversations.

1. Make the invoice easy to pay in the first place

Many payment delays start with the invoice itself. Before you chase anyone, check that every invoice you send includes:

  • A unique invoice number and the date it was issued.
  • A clear due date ("Due 15 November"), not just "Net 30".
  • The client's purchase order or project reference, if they gave you one.
  • Exactly what the payment is for, and the total.
  • How to pay: bank details, a payment link, or both.
  • The right recipient. Ask early whether invoices go to your contact or to an accounts email.

If your payment terms include a late fee, put that on the invoice and in your contract too. A fee that wasn't agreed in advance is hard to enforce and can damage the relationship.

2. Know exactly who owes you what

You can't follow up on time if you don't notice an invoice is late. Keep one list of every invoice with its due date and status, and check it once a week. Sorting by "days overdue" shows you who to contact first. Here is an example for a freelancer checking their list on 1 November:

InvoiceClientDue dateDays overdueAmount
#041Studio A2 Oct30$1,200
#044Shop B22 Oct10$850
#045Agency C29 Oct3$2,400
#046Café D8 Novnot yet due$600
Total outstanding$5,050

Of the $5,050 outstanding, $4,450 is already overdue ($1,200 + $850 + $2,400) and $600 isn't due yet. Each overdue invoice is at a different stage, so each one gets a different message. If you don't have a list like this yet, our guide on how to track invoices as a freelancer shows how to set one up.

3. Follow a simple reminder schedule

A fixed schedule takes the emotion out of chasing money. You're not "being pushy"; you're running your normal process. This is a common pattern you can adapt:

WhenWhat to sendTone
3 days before dueFriendly heads-up with the invoice attachedHelpful
Due dateShort note that payment is due todayNeutral
3 days overdueFirst reminder, ask if anything is missingPolite
10 days overdueSecond reminder, ask for a payment dateFirm
30 days overdueFinal notice, state next stepsFormal

Before the due date

"Hi Sam, just a quick note that invoice #046 for $600 is due on 8 November. I've attached it again here for convenience. Thanks!" This catches invoices that went to the wrong person while there is still time to fix it.

3 days overdue

"Hi Sam, I wanted to check in on invoice #045 for $2,400, which was due on 29 October. Could you let me know if you need anything else from me to process it?" Asking whether something is missing gives the client an easy, face-saving reply and often reveals the real problem, like a missing PO number.

10 days overdue

"Hi Sam, invoice #044 for $850 is now 10 days past due. Could you confirm the date payment will be sent?" Asking for a specific date turns a vague "we'll look into it" into a commitment you can follow up on.

30 days overdue

"Hi Sam, invoice #041 for $1,200 is now 30 days overdue. Please arrange payment by 8 November. If I don't receive it by then, I'll need to pause further work and apply the late fee in our agreement." Only mention steps you are prepared to take, and only fees that are in your contract.

4. Change channels when email goes quiet

If two emails get no answer, the problem may be the inbox, not the client. Try a short phone call or message to your main contact, then email the accounts or finance team directly with the invoice attached. Keep every message polite and factual; you may want to forward the thread later.

5. Work out any late fee before you mention it

If your contract includes a late fee, calculate it so your message is precise. As an example only, a contract with a 1.5% monthly late fee on the $1,200 invoice would add $1,200 × 1.5% = $18 for one month late, making the total $1,218. Rules on late fees and interest differ by country and state, so check what applies to you. Many freelancers offer to waive the fee if the client pays by a set date; that often gets money moving faster than the fee itself.

6. Know when to stop work, and when to escalate

If an invoice is still unpaid after the final notice, you have a few options. You can pause any ongoing work for that client, offer a payment plan (for example, two payments of $600 to clear the $1,200 invoice), or look into a formal route such as a small-claims process or a collection service. Those options vary by location and have their own costs, so find out what applies to you before committing. For future projects with the same client, ask for a deposit up front.

7. Protect your budget while you wait

Late payments hurt most when your bills depend on them. Only count money as income once it lands in your account, keep a small cash buffer for slow months, and set aside tax from each payment as it arrives rather than from what you've invoiced. If your income is uneven, our guide on budgeting on irregular income walks through a baseline-month approach.

Tip: put follow-ups on your calendar the day you send each invoice: one reminder 3 days before the due date, and one each for 3, 10 and 30 days after. When the payment arrives, delete the rest. It takes a minute and means nothing slips through.
