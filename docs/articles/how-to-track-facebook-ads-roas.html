How to Track Facebook Ads ROAS (and Know Your Break-Even Number)

Updated 2026-11-07 · 7 min read

A campaign with a "good" ROAS can still lose money, and a campaign that looks average can be your most profitable one. The difference is knowing your own break-even number and tracking results in one place, week by week, instead of glancing at Ads Manager and guessing.

What ROAS means

ROAS stands for return on ad spend. It answers one question: for every dollar you put into ads, how many dollars of sales came back?

ROAS = revenue from ads ÷ ad spend

If you spend $200 on a Facebook or Instagram campaign and it brings in $600 of sales, your ROAS is $600 ÷ $200 = 3.0 (often written 3x or 300%). That number alone doesn't tell you whether you made money, though. Revenue is not profit. You still have to pay for the product, shipping, payment fees and packaging.

Step 1: Work out your break-even ROAS

Your break-even ROAS is the point where ad spend eats exactly all of your profit. Below it, every sale from ads loses money. Above it, ads are paying for themselves.

First, find your margin per order before ad costs:

Per orderAmount
Selling price$50
Product cost−$15
Shipping, packaging and payment fees−$10
Margin before ads$25

$25 ÷ $50 = a 50% margin. Then:

Break-even ROAS = 1 ÷ margin = 1 ÷ 0.50 = 2.0

So for this shop, any campaign under 2.0 ROAS is losing money and anything above it is profitable. A shop with a 25% margin would need a ROAS of 4.0 just to break even, which is why copying someone else's "target ROAS" from a forum rarely works. Use your own numbers.

Step 2: Set up a simple tracking sheet

Ads Manager shows a lot of columns. For tracking, you only need a few, recorded once a week per campaign:

  • Date range (for example, Mon–Sun)
  • Campaign name
  • Amount spent
  • Purchases (number of orders)
  • Revenue (purchase conversion value)
  • ROAS = revenue ÷ spend
  • Profit after ads = revenue × margin − spend

Copy the spend, purchases and value from Ads Manager into your sheet every Monday. Let formulas calculate ROAS and profit so you never do the division by hand.

Overall, the account turned $700 of ads into $1,700 of sales, a ROAS of about 2.43, and $150 of profit after ad costs. But the campaigns are very different:

  • A is clearly profitable. It's a candidate for a careful budget increase.
  • B is exactly at break-even. It isn't losing money, but it isn't earning any either. Test new creatives before spending more.
  • C lost $75. One week of data on a new campaign is not much, so give it a fixed test budget and a deadline, then cut it if it stays below 2.0.

Step 4: Check Ads Manager against real sales

The revenue Meta reports is based on its attribution settings (by default, sales within a set number of days after a click or view). It can count a sale that would have happened anyway, or miss a sale that a customer made on another device. So once a week, also compare:

Total store revenue ÷ total ad spend

Some people call this the blended ROAS or MER. If your shop made $2,100 in total sales in the week above, the blended figure is $2,100 ÷ $700 = 3.0. If Ads Manager says one thing and your store totals say something very different over several weeks, trust the store numbers for big decisions and treat the campaign-level ROAS as a guide to which ads work better than others.

Step 5: Make decisions on a schedule, not on a mood

Ad results bounce around from day to day. Checking every hour leads to panicked changes. A calmer routine:

  1. Daily (2 minutes): only check that ads are running and spend isn't far above plan.
  2. Weekly (15 minutes): fill in the sheet, compare each campaign's ROAS with your break-even number, and write one note per campaign: keep, test, or stop.
  3. Monthly: add up ad spend, revenue and profit after ads for the month. This is the number that tells you whether advertising is helping the business grow.

A common guideline is to change budgets in small steps (for example, 20% or less at a time) and to give a new campaign a few days of data before judging it. These are rules of thumb, not guarantees, so watch how your own account responds.

Tip: recalculate your break-even ROAS whenever your costs change. If your supplier raises the product cost from $15 to $20, the margin in the example falls to $20 (40%) and break-even ROAS rises from 2.0 to 2.5. Campaigns that were profitable last month can quietly slip below the line.

Common mistakes

  • Judging ROAS without your margin. A 2.5 ROAS is great at 60% margin and a loss at 30%.
  • Forgetting refunds and returns. Subtract them from revenue in your sheet, or your ROAS will look better than reality.
  • Mixing ad costs with other spending. Keep ad spend as its own category in your books so your monthly profit is accurate. Our guide to small business bookkeeping for beginners shows a simple way to set that up.
  • Scaling too fast. Doubling a budget overnight often changes results. Increase gradually and keep tracking.

Paid ads always carry risk, and results depend on your product, prices and audience. This guide is general information to help you measure what's happening, not advice on how much to spend.
