Small Business Bookkeeping for Beginners: A Simple Monthly System

Updated 2026-11-06 · 7 min read

Bookkeeping sounds like accountant territory, but for a small shop, side business or one-person service it comes down to one habit: write down every bit of money that comes in and goes out, and check the totals once a month. Here's a beginner system you can set up in an afternoon and keep up in about 30 minutes a month.

What bookkeeping actually is (and isn't)

Bookkeeping is the record of your business money: what you sold, what you spent, and what's left. Accounting is what happens with those records later: tax returns, financial statements, planning. If your bookkeeping is clean, everything else gets easier and usually cheaper, because an accountant spends less time sorting out a messy shoebox of receipts.

You don't need special software to start. A spreadsheet with a few columns works fine for most small businesses with a modest number of transactions each month.

Step 1: Separate business and personal money

This is the most useful thing you can do. Open a separate bank account (and, if you use one, a separate card) only for the business. Every sale goes into it, every business cost comes out of it. When you pay yourself, move money from the business account to your personal account and record it as an owner's draw or salary, depending on how your business is set up.

Mixing the two is the number one reason beginners can't tell whether their business is making money. One account means your bank statement is already half of your bookkeeping.

Step 2: Pick a short list of categories

Every transaction gets a category. Keep the list short, around 8 to 12 categories, so it's quick to use. A typical starter list:

  • Income: sales, services, shipping charged to customers, other income
  • Expenses: materials or stock, shipping and packaging, platform and payment fees, software and subscriptions, advertising, phone and internet (business share), equipment, professional fees

Check which expense categories your local tax forms use and match them where you can. That way, your year-end totals drop straight into your return.

Step 3: Record every transaction

Your record (often called a ledger) needs one row per transaction with these columns: date, description, category, money in, money out, and a receipt reference. For example: "12 Nov · 200 mailer boxes · Shipping & packaging · $0 · $64 · R-031".

Pick a rhythm and stick to it. Recording once a week takes about 10 minutes; leaving it for three months can take a whole weekend. Payment platforms and banks let you download transactions as a file, which you can paste in and then add categories.

Step 4: Keep receipts and invoices

Every expense should have proof: a receipt, an invoice or an order confirmation. Snap a photo or save the PDF, name it with the same reference as the ledger row (R-031), and keep it in one folder per month. For your own sales, keep a copy of each invoice you send. How long you need to keep records depends on where you are; several years is common, so check the rules that apply to you.

Step 5: Close each month in 30 minutes

At the start of each month, do a short "close" of the previous one:

  1. Make sure every bank transaction from last month is in your ledger with a category.
  2. Check that every expense row has a receipt reference.
  3. Reconcile: your ledger's closing balance should match the bank statement.
  4. Look at the monthly summary (income, expenses, profit) and compare it with last month.
  5. Move money for tax into a separate savings account.

A worked example: one month for a small online shop

Here's a month for a small shop selling handmade products online, summarised by category:

CategoryTypeAmount
Product salesIncome$3,850
Shipping charged to customersIncome$150
Total income$4,000
MaterialsExpense$1,240
Shipping & packagingExpense$310
Platform & payment feesExpense$260
Software & subscriptionsExpense$85
AdvertisingExpense$400
Phone & internet (business share)Expense$65
Total expenses$2,360
Profit for the month$1,640

$4,000 − $2,360 = $1,640 profit. If this owner sets aside 25% of profit for tax as a personal guideline, that's $1,640 × 0.25 = $410 moved to a tax savings account, leaving $1,230. The right percentage depends on your country, income and business type, so treat any rule of thumb as a starting point and confirm it with a tax professional. Then the reconciliation check against the bank account:

Bank checkAmount
Opening balance (1st of the month)$2,500
+ Money in (total income)$4,000
− Money out (total expenses)$2,360
Expected closing balance$4,140

If the bank statement shows $4,140 at month end, the books balance. If it doesn't, the gap is usually a missing transaction, a duplicate row, or a fee the platform took before paying you out.

Profit isn't the same as cash

Beginners are often surprised when the business shows a profit but the bank balance feels tight. Common reasons: you bought stock this month that you'll sell next month, a customer hasn't paid an invoice yet, or you paid yourself. Watching both numbers, profit and bank balance, gives you the full picture.

Common beginner mistakes

  • Recording only sales. Small expenses like fees and subscriptions add up, and missing them overstates your profit.
  • Recording payouts instead of sales. If a platform pays you $950 after taking $50 in fees, record $1,000 of sales and $50 of fees, not just $950.
  • No receipts. An expense without proof can be hard to justify later.
  • Waiting until tax time. A monthly close keeps the job small and lets you spot problems early.
  • Too many categories. If choosing a category takes thought, merge some.
Tip: set a recurring 30-minute calendar slot on the first working day of every month called "Close the books". Starting fresh in January? Set up your categories in December so the new year begins with clean records.
