How to Budget for Car Expenses: The Real Monthly Cost of Owning a Car

Updated 2026-11-13 · 6 min read

Most people budget for their car payment and fuel, then get caught out by everything else: the insurance renewal, new tires, the registration fee, the repair nobody saw coming. The fix is simple. Add up what your car really costs in a year, divide by 12, and set that amount aside every month.

Why car costs break so many budgets

A car has two kinds of costs. Some arrive every month, like a loan payment or fuel. Others arrive once or twice a year, often in large amounts: insurance paid annually or every six months, registration, inspections, tires, brakes. Because those bigger bills don't show up in a normal month, they're easy to forget, and when they land they get pushed onto a credit card.

If you only budget for the monthly part, your budget looks fine for ten months and falls apart in the other two. The goal of this guide is to turn the whole year of car spending into one steady monthly number.

Step 1: List every car cost you have

Go through the last 12 months of bank and card statements and pull out anything car-related. If you don't have a full year of records, use your best estimates and adjust later. A typical list looks like this:

  • Car payment or lease (if you have one)
  • Fuel or charging
  • Insurance
  • Routine maintenance: oil changes, tire rotations, filters, wiper blades
  • Registration, licence and inspection fees
  • Repairs: brakes, battery, tires, anything that breaks
  • Parking, tolls and car washes

Don't skip the small items. Parking and tolls often add up to more than people expect over a full year.

Step 2: Turn everything into a yearly total

Convert each line into a yearly amount. Monthly costs get multiplied by 12; annual bills stay as they are. Here's a worked example for one car, with the loan payment kept separate for now:

Car costHow it's paidPer yearPer month
FuelAbout $150 a month$1,800$150
InsuranceTwice a year, $600 each$1,200$100
Routine maintenanceOil changes, rotations, filters$480$40
Registration & inspectionOnce a year$180$15
Repairs bufferUnpredictable$600$50
Parking & tollsAbout $30 a month$360$30
Total running costs$4,620$385

So this car costs $385 a month to run, before any loan payment. If the owner also pays $350 a month on a car loan, the true monthly cost of the car is $350 + $385 = $735. That is the number that belongs in the budget, not just $350 plus fuel.

Step 3: Pick a repairs number you can live with

Repairs are the hardest line to estimate. A few ways to set it:

  • Look back at what you spent on repairs over the last two or three years and take the yearly average.
  • If the car is newer and under warranty, a smaller buffer may be enough.
  • If the car is older or has high mileage, set a bigger buffer. Older cars tend to need more work, and the bills tend to be larger.

There's no perfect number. The point is to have something set aside so a $400 brake job is an inconvenience, not a crisis. If the buffer builds up unused, that's fine: it can help with tires later or go toward your next car.

Step 4: Split the monthly amount by how it's spent

Not all of that $385 gets spent the same way. Fuel and parking go out every week. The rest should sit in a separate savings account, often called a sinking fund, until the bill arrives. Using the example above:

Where the money goes each monthAmount
Car loan payment (from checking)$350
Fuel (spending money)$150
Car sinking fund: insurance $100 + maintenance $40 + registration $15 + repairs $50 + parking $30$235
Total each month$735

Parking is listed in the sinking fund here because some people pay for it in larger chunks, such as a monthly or yearly permit. If you pay as you go, move it to the spending side instead. The total doesn't change.

Set up an automatic transfer of the sinking fund amount on payday. When the insurance renewal or registration notice comes in, you pay it from that account and your regular budget doesn't feel it.

Step 5: Check it against your income

Once you know the real monthly cost, compare it with your take-home pay. Some people use a guideline of keeping total transport costs to a modest share of take-home pay, but there's no single right percentage: it depends on where you live, how far you commute and whether you have other options like public transport.

A simpler test: after paying for the car, can you still cover housing, food, bills and some saving each month? If not, the car may be costing more than your budget can carry. Our free paycheck budget calculator can help you see where the $735 fits alongside everything else.

Ways to lower your car costs

  • Shop your insurance at renewal time. Getting a few quotes, raising the deductible (if your repairs fund can cover it) or bundling policies can lower the premium.
  • Keep up with maintenance. Regular oil changes and correct tire pressure are cheap compared with the repairs they help prevent.
  • Combine trips and drive smoothly to use less fuel.
  • Pay annual bills in full if your insurer charges extra for monthly instalments, using the sinking fund.
  • Think about the next car early. Saving toward it now can mean a smaller loan, or none at all, next time.

Review it once a year

Fuel prices, insurance premiums and repair needs change. Once a year, ideally when you set up your yearly budget, look back at what the car actually cost and update each line. If you spent much more on repairs than planned, raise the buffer. If fuel dropped because you work from home more, lower it and move the difference to a goal.
