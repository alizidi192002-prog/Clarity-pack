How to Calculate Your Net Worth: A Simple Step-by-Step Guide

Updated 2026-11-04 · 6 min read

Your budget tells you where this month's money goes. Your net worth tells you where all of it has gone so far. It's one number: everything you own minus everything you owe. It takes about 20 minutes to work out, and tracking it a few times a year shows whether your money habits are actually moving you forward.

What net worth means (and what it doesn't)

Net worth is a snapshot, not a score. A negative number is common, especially if you have student loans or recently bought a car, and it doesn't mean you're doing badly. What matters most is the direction: is the number going up over time? Comparing yourself with other people is far less useful than comparing yourself with your own number from six months ago.

The formula is simple:

Net worth = total assets − total liabilities

1. Gather your numbers in one sitting

Open your banking app, any savings or investment accounts, and your loan and credit card statements. Write down the balance of each one on the same day. Mixing balances from different weeks makes the result less accurate, because money moves between accounts.

2. List your assets (what you own)

Assets are things with money value that you could, in principle, turn into cash. The usual ones:

  • Cash: checking accounts, savings accounts, emergency fund, cash at home.
  • Investments: retirement accounts, brokerage accounts, any other investments at their current value.
  • Property: your home at a realistic sale price, not what you hope it's worth.
  • Vehicles: what you could sell the car for today, not what you paid.

Leave out everyday belongings like furniture, clothes and electronics. They lose value quickly and are hard to price, so including them makes the number look better without telling you anything useful. A good guideline: only include something if you could reasonably sell it for a known price.

3. List your liabilities (what you owe)

Liabilities are all your debts, at the balance still owed today:

  • Mortgage balance
  • Car loans
  • Student loans
  • Credit card balances (the full balance, even if you pay it off monthly)
  • Personal loans, buy-now-pay-later plans, and money owed to family

Use the remaining balance, not the original loan amount or the monthly payment.

4. Subtract: a worked example

Here's what this looks like for someone renting, with a car loan, a credit card balance and student loans:

AssetsValue
Checking account$2,400
Savings / emergency fund$6,500
Retirement account$18,200
Car (current resale value)$9,000
Total assets$36,100
LiabilitiesBalance owed
Car loan$7,300
Credit card$1,850
Student loan$14,600
Total liabilities$23,750

$36,100 − $23,750 = $12,350 net worth.

Notice the car: it's worth $9,000 but $7,300 is still owed, so it only adds $1,700 to net worth. Looking at assets and debts side by side like this often changes how big purchases feel.

5. Track it every quarter

One calculation is interesting. Several in a row are useful. Pick four dates a year, such as the first day of January, April, July and October, and repeat the same steps with the same list of accounts. Continuing the example:

DateNet worthChange
1 January$12,350—
1 April$13,900+$1,550
1 July$15,200+$1,300
1 October$16,050+$850
Change over 9 months+$3,700

The growth slowed in the third quarter. That's the kind of thing worth a closer look: maybe a summer trip, a car repair, or a new subscription. The number won't tell you why, but it tells you where to look in your budget.

Monthly tracking is fine too, but quarterly is usually enough. Investment values move up and down day to day, and checking too often can make normal swings feel like problems.

6. Use the number to decide what to do next

Your net worth sheet shows two levers: grow assets or shrink debts. A few practical ways to read it:

  • Little or no cash savings? Building a small emergency fund usually comes first, so a surprise bill doesn't go on a credit card. See how to build an emergency fund.
  • High-interest debt on the list? Paying it down raises your net worth by the amount paid plus the interest you no longer pay. The free debt snowball and avalanche calculator shows which order clears it fastest.
  • Debt shrinking but net worth flat? Check whether new debt or a falling asset (like a car losing value) is cancelling out your progress.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Using purchase prices: a $25,000 car bought two years ago isn't worth $25,000 now. Check a realistic resale value.
  • Forgetting small debts: store cards, buy-now-pay-later plans and money owed to friends all count.
  • Changing the list each time: if you add or remove accounts between checks, the comparison stops being fair. Keep the same list and add new accounts as they appear.
  • Treating it as a judgement: it's a measuring tool. A falling number during a hard year is information, not failure.
Tip: put your first net worth check on 1 January. It gives you a clear starting point for the year, and your 2027 progress will be easy to see by December.
